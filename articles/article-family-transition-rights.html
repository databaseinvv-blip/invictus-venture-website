FAMILY TRANSITION · 12 MAY 2026 · 9 MIN READ

Who Runs the Company, and Who Just Owns It

The uncle who built the brand. The cousin who inherited shares but lives overseas. The son-in-law who has run operations for fifteen years and owns nothing. A family business has a kind of decision-making no other company faces — and one distinction decides whether the handover survives.

A family business carries a tension no ordinary company has. In a normal company, authority follows contribution and ownership. In a family company, two other hierarchies sit on top: age and bloodline. The uncle who built the brand. The cousin who inherited shares but lives abroad and has never worked a day in the business. The son-in-law who has run operations for fifteen years and holds no equity at all. The patriarch who is well past retirement but still chairs every meeting. Everyone has a claim, and the claims do not agree.

Where transitions actually break

Most people assume a family transition fails at the legal stage — a badly drafted will, a missing clause. In our experience it rarely does. It breaks earlier and more quietly, because nobody ever wrote down which rules of decision-making apply, and to whom. Who gets a vote on operations? Who only gets a share of the profit? Who can block a sale? When that is left unspoken, the first serious disagreement turns into a family rupture, and the business is collateral damage.

Effort earns rights. Bloodline keeps dignity. The company keeps running. All three can be true at once — but only if they are written down.

The distinction that solves most of it

The single most useful move in a family transition is to separate two things that families instinctively bundle together: operational rights and economic rights. Once you stop treating them as one package, almost every awkward case becomes manageable.

Operational rights — for the people who run it

Active contributors — the family members actually building and running the business — receive equity carrying operational rights: voting, board seats, day-to-day control. They steer the company because they are the ones in it. This is how you make sure the son-in-law who has run operations for fifteen years is not outvoted by a cousin who has never been to the office.

Economic rights — for the family who own it

Non-active family members receive equity carrying economic rights: a share of dividends, capital gain, beneficiary status. They benefit from the company's success — their stake is real and valuable — but they do not direct operations they are not part of. Their dignity and their inheritance are protected, without handing them control over decisions they are not equipped to make.

Both sides keep what matters to them. The operators keep the ability to run the business well. The wider family keeps a fair share of what the business produces. Nobody is disinherited, and nobody who isn't running the company gets to paralyse the people who are.

The tools that make it hold

This separation is delivered through a small set of structures, designed together rather than bolted on one at a time:

  • A family constitution — the written rules of decision-making: who decides what, how disputes are handled, how the next generation enters, what happens when someone wants out.
  • Dual-class shares — the mechanism that lets operational rights and economic rights live in different share classes, so contribution and bloodline can each be honoured without colliding.
  • Generational succession sequencing — a planned handover over time, rather than a single dramatic event triggered by illness or death, so the company never faces a sudden vacuum at the top.

Why time is the hidden risk

The most expensive version of this problem is the one that is never addressed until it is forced — when the founder falls ill, or passes, and the family discovers in the worst possible moment that nothing was written down. At that point every conversation is emotional, every interpretation is contested, and the business often cannot wait for the family to resolve it. A transition designed calmly, years ahead, costs a fraction of the dispute it prevents — and it lets the founder shape the outcome rather than leaving it to chance and grief.

The aim is not to choose between the family and the business. It is to build a structure where the people who run the company can keep running it, the family who own it keep their fair share and their standing, and the handover from one generation to the next is something the company survives intact — ideally something it never even feels.
