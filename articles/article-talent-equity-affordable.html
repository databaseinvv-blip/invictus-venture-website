TALENT EQUITY & ESOS · 20 MAY 2026 · 9 MIN READ

Your Best Manager Can’t Afford to Buy In. Design It So She Can.

A profitable outlet, a manager who runs it better than you do, and an offer to make her a part-owner. Then the valuation lands: her 20% costs more than a house. Here is how good talent-equity design closes the gap between the offer and what the person can pay.

Most owners reach the same moment eventually. Someone in the business — a branch manager, a head of operations, the person who quietly makes the whole thing run — has become too important to lose. You decide to give them a real stake. Not a bonus. Ownership. And then the structure defeats the good intention, because nobody designed the entry the person could actually afford.

A real example of the gap

Consider a single outlet doing well: six treatment beds, fully booked, two years old, around RM300,000 a month in revenue at a 40% net margin — roughly RM120,000 of profit a month. By a sensible valuation method, that outlet is worth several million ringgit. Good news for the owner. Then comes the offer to the manager who runs it: own 20% of this.

The manager who deserves the equity cannot write a seven-figure cheque. The offer dies on contact with the price. This is the single most common reason talent-equity plans quietly fail: the math is correct, but it ignores who is actually meant to buy in.

The right valuation is not the highest defensible number. It is the number the right person can actually pay.

The fix is structure, not generosity

You do not solve this by guessing a discount or by gifting shares and hoping for the best. You solve it with deliberate design across a few moving parts:

  • A defensible valuation first. Before you can price an entry, you need a share value that holds up — not a number pulled from the air. For a company with no public market, that requires a proper non-listed valuation methodology, which is exactly what an SME ESOS depends on.
  • An entry price matched to the buyer. Once the value is set, the subscription terms can be designed so the key person enters at a price they can fund — through a fair discount, staged subscription, or earn-in — without the owner feeling they gave it away.
  • Performance vesting, not a free gift. Equity that arrives the moment it is promised teaches nothing. Equity that vests against milestones rewards the contribution you actually wanted, and protects you if the person leaves early.

Two different groups, two different tools

Not everyone in the business should be rewarded the same way, and conflating them is another reason these plans fail.

The revenue drivers (Pillar C — Talent Equity)

These are the people whose contribution is measurable: the manager whose outlet performance you can point to, the commercial lead whose number is visible. For them, the work is translating a commission mindset into ownership — converting "what did I sell this quarter" into "what is this company worth in five years." Their equity is tied to results, with vesting and lock-up so retention does not become a trap, and an exit path so the upside is real rather than theoretical.

Everyone who keeps it running (Pillar D — ESOS for SMEs)

Then there is everyone else the business depends on but whose contribution is harder to put a number on — operations, support, admin, the backend. Their work does not show up as a sales figure, but the company stops without them. An ESOS designed for a non-listed SME lets the whole company participate, with subscription mechanics that spell out what people pay, when they pay, and how the scheme is administered. The point is alignment: every person holding a stake now has the same goal as the owner.

Why “just give them shares” backfires

Owners often try the shortcut — hand over some percentage and move on. It tends to go wrong in predictable ways. There is no agreed price, so nobody knows what the equity is worth or what it costs to exit. There is no vesting, so a person can leave in year two holding a stake they did not earn. There is no liquidity design, so the shares are a certificate that never converts to cash and the “reward” quietly breeds resentment. A real structure answers all of these before the first share changes hands.

What the work looks like

  • Valuation — a defensible share price for a company with no public market.
  • Entry design — subscription terms the intended owner can actually fund.
  • Vesting & lock-up — structures that retain without trapping, with clean departure mechanics built in.
  • Exit liquidity — secondary windows or redemption mechanics so the scheme genuinely pays out.

Done well, the conversation changes. Instead of an offer your best people cannot accept, you have a structure that turns the staff who build your company into owners who think like you do — which is the entire point of giving equity in the first place.
