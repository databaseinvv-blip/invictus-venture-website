Why a Dingy Corner Shop Is Worth RM10 Million

Walk past one of the country's best-known minimarts and you do not think 'convenience store.' You think 'ten-million-ringgit store.' That instinct — not the profit on the shelves — is the asset. Here is how a business builds it.

There is a grocer somewhere near you that probably out-earns a single outlet of the country's largest minimart chain. Same street, more profit per month. And yet one is worth a fortune and the other is worth almost nothing the day the owner tries to sell. The difference is not how much money each makes. It is how much each is worth — and those are two completely different questions.

A single dingy unit, valued at around ten million ringgit. Not because of what it earns — the corner grocer may earn more — but because of what the network behind it represents. We break that down with three letters: S, S, B.

S — Scalability

The chain does not add stores. It multiplies them. Roughly two hundred and eighty new outlets a year over a decade is, in effect, one new store every working day. That pace is only possible because everything is standardised — layout, range, fit-out, supplier terms. There is no negotiation per store. You take the standard or you don't open. A business that grows by addition has a ceiling. A business that grows by multiplication is what capital markets pay for.

The "free store" trick

Here is the part most operators miss. Each new store is close to free to open, because the model finances itself:

  • Deposit only on the premises — no heavy upfront capital expenditure.
  • Minimal renovation — pull in power, paint, install shelving, open.
  • Supplier terms of around 90 days — sell the stock and collect the cash before the supplier is paid. The inventory funds itself.
  • Contractor terms — hand a contractor hundreds of stores to build on a one-month payment term, and the build is effectively pre-financed too.

Net cost per store comes down to roughly the tenancy deposit — tens of thousands of ringgit, not the millions a typical retail build-out swallows. Cheap-to-replicate growth is the engine under the valuation.

S — Sustainability

The second question a buyer asks: will this business still matter in ten years? Convenience retail at this footprint will. Compare that to the businesses that erupt and vanish inside two years — the bubble tea wave is the obvious example. Explosive growth that cannot last attracts a low multiple, because the buyer is pricing in the decline. Durability is worth a premium precisely because most businesses do not have it.

Another grocer's single shop may earn more than one of theirs. But their PE multiple is in a different universe.

B — Brand influence

This is not branding, and it is not advertising — the chain barely advertises. Brand influence is the power its name has already won in your head. When you pass one of these stores you do not register "a shop." You register a category, a price point, a benchmark. That automatic recognition is a moat. Competitors cannot buy their way past it quickly, because it was built store by store, year by year, in millions of small daily impressions.

Making money vs being worth money

Bring the three together and the ten-million-ringgit figure stops being a mystery. The market is not paying for last month's profit on the shelves. It is paying for a machine that can multiply cheaply (Scalability), that will still be wanted in a decade (Sustainability), and whose name already carries weight (Brand influence). Profit is the symptom. SSB is the cause.

This is the uncomfortable mirror for most owners. You can run a genuinely profitable shop for twenty years and discover, on the day you try to sell, that it is worth very little — because it scores low on all three. It earns money. It is not worth money. The good news is that all three are design choices, not luck. They can be built into a business deliberately, from far earlier than most founders think.

Where we start

  • Scalability audit — is your growth additive or multiplicative, and what is stopping it from replicating cheaply?
  • Sustainability test — how a buyer would price the next ten years, not the last one.
  • Brand-influence map — what your name actually means in the market today, and what it would need to mean to command a premium.

The aim is simple: to move your business from one that merely makes money to one that is genuinely worth money — so that the number a buyer sees matches the company you actually built.
