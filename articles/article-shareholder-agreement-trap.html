The RM1.5 Million That Could Not Move

Two clauses. Each looked harmless on its own. Together they locked RM1.5 million in a bank account, paralysed a profitable company, and produced four court cases in a single year. This is the case that started everything we do.

Most founders believe a shareholder agreement is about percentages. Who owns what. The truth is harder and more useful: a shareholder agreement is decided by the partner with the higher level of understanding, regardless of who owns more. We learned this the most expensive way possible.

Three founders. Roughly a third of the equity each. One had come from a private-equity background; one was the public-facing partner; one was an accountant who managed the money. On paper it was balanced and sensible. The company was profitable and had just raised RM1.5 million from outside investors. The money sat in the company's bank account, waiting to be deployed.

Then the partnership broke down. Not because anyone was evil — because the people at the table did not understand the combined effect of two clauses they had all signed.

The first clause: who can remove a director

The agreement said that a director could only be removed by the person who appointed them. It reads like fairness. In practice, the accountant had appointed himself. That single sentence meant the other two founders — even acting together, even with a clear majority — could not remove him. He had made himself permanent.

The second clause: who must sign

The second clause named him the compulsory signatory on company cheques. Every payment needed his signature. He held under a third of the shares, but no ringgit could leave the company without him.

Read each clause alone and a competent lawyer would wave it through. Read them together and you have built something else entirely: a minority shareholder with total control over the company's two most important functions — its board and its bank account.

Each clause alone looks fine. Together they turn a one-third shareholder into a one-hundred-percent gatekeeper.

What happened when the relationship soured

Feeling sidelined, the accountant moved to protect himself. He set up a parallel company in the same line of business, raised money into it, and used the original company's name on his materials. When the other two confronted him, he simply stopped cooperating. And because of the two clauses, there was nothing they could do.

  • They tried to remove him as a director. Blocked by the first clause — only he could remove himself.
  • They tried to dilute him. Blocked, because the bank refused to activate the resolution without his signature.
  • They tried to open a new bank account elsewhere. They could — but the RM1.5 million was already sitting in the original account, and it could not be moved.

For a full year the business was paralysed. Investors called daily asking what was happening. The founders spent every day fighting instead of building.

The judge's three outcomes

Eventually a judge looked at the mountain of evidence and reduced the entire dispute to one sentence that we still teach today: there are only three possible endings. You buy him, he buys you, or the company is wound up. There is no fourth door. Every shareholder dispute, no matter how it begins, collapses into those three outcomes.

The lesson worth more than the loss

The accountant was not a criminal mastermind. He used clauses everyone had agreed to. The damage came from a gap in understanding — the others did not see how two ordinary provisions would combine. That is the real risk in a shareholder agreement. It is rarely a single dramatic clause. It is the interaction between several reasonable ones.

This is why we say a shareholder agreement is won by the higher level of understanding. You do not need to be a lawyer or an accountant to be protected. You need someone at the table who can see how the clauses behave together, under stress, when goodwill is gone — because that is the only moment the agreement is ever actually used.

What we look for in your agreement

  • Director removal mechanics — who can remove whom, and whether any single party has made themselves unremovable.
  • Signatory and banking control — whether financial control matches equity, or quietly overrides it.
  • Deadlock and exit terms — the buy-sell mechanism that decides the price and process before anyone is angry.
  • Combination risk — the clause-against-clause stress test that almost no standard template performs.

The cost of getting this right at the start is a fraction of one month of the dispute it prevents. We know, because the firm exists because of a dispute that was not prevented.
