Your Bubble Tea Shop Will Never Be a Listed Company

It feels rich. The margin on every cup is fantastic, the queue is real, the cash is good. So why will it never become anything bigger? Because the thing that makes it feel rich is the very thing that caps what it can ever be worth.

Article Cover
1680 × 720

A good drinks shop is one of the most satisfying small businesses to run. The margin on each cup is high, the cash comes in daily, the queue out front feels like proof you have made it. And almost none of them ever become a large, valuable, sellable company. Understanding why is one of the most important lessons an owner can learn — because the trap is invisible while the money is good.

Margin feels like wealth. It isn't scale.

The reason the shop feels rich is its margin per cup. That is real money, and it is worth having. But a buyer or an investor is not pricing your margin. They are pricing whether the business can multiply. And a single high-margin shop, however good, runs straight into a wall the moment you try to grow it.

The franchise math that quietly fails

The usual answer to "how do I grow?" is to franchise. Here is where the arithmetic turns against you. As a franchisor you no longer earn the full margin of a shop — you earn fees and a royalty, often a single-digit percentage of each franchisee's sales.

Twenty shops, each paying you roughly six percent, sums to about a hundred and twenty percent. You have handed twenty operators the bulk of the economics, taken on brand risk across twenty locations, and ended up with not much more than running one shop yourself. The model spreads effort far faster than it spreads reward.

Margin is not scalability. And capital markets pay for the second, not the first.

What "scalable" actually means

A business scales when each additional unit is cheaper and easier than the last, and the economics stay with the owner of the system. That requires standardisation deep enough that the next outlet does not depend on the founder's personal magic. If the first shop's success is really you — your taste, your relationships, your presence — you do not have a scalable business. You have a very good job that pays well and cannot be sold.

If, instead, the success lives in a system — recipes, training, supply terms, layout, the things that make outlet number twenty perform like outlet number one — then every new unit is lower effort, and the whole thing becomes worth far more than the sum of its monthly profits.

The test before you fall in love with margin

So before you celebrate the margin, ask the harder questions:

  • Can it replicate? Would store two perform like store one without you standing in it?
  • Who keeps the economics as it grows? Does scale concentrate value with you, or leak it to franchisees and fees?
  • Will it still be wanted in ten years? Or is it riding a wave that contracts as fast as it rose?
  • Is the magic the founder, or the system? Only one of those is sellable.

Two different goals, two different businesses

None of this means a high-margin shop is a bad business. If your goal is good income and a life you enjoy, a great single shop can deliver exactly that for years. The mistake is assuming that income will one day convert into a large sale or a listing. It usually won't — not because you ran it badly, but because it was never structured to multiply.

If being worth money — not just making it — is the goal, then scalability has to be designed in from early, before the comfort of the margin makes the question feel unnecessary. That design work is most of what we do: turning a business that earns into a business that is worth something to someone else.
